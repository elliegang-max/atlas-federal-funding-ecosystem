Atlas

A map of the federal funding ecosystem for university spinouts
MGT 409 — Final Project

Author Ellie Gang
Repo github.com/elliegang-max/atlas-federal-funding-ecosystem
App Runs locally — pip install -r requirements.txt then python app.py, see README
Date October 26, 2026

Atlas maps the federal funding ecosystem as a clickable graph of agencies, venture arms, check sizes and named contacts — one interface for a tech transfer office building relationships, and a second where a researcher describes their science and gets ranked, sourced funding matches.

2Motivation

Who has the problem

Yale Ventures runs Yale's technology commercialization, and inside it the Blavatnik Fund for Innovation is an early-stage fund backing pharmaceutical, biotech, medical device and health-technology ventures coming out of Yale labs. Its leadership asked a question I spent the summer of 2026 working on: how do we understand the federal funding ecosystem well enough to know how each agency relates to every other one, and who we should be building relationships with before we need them?

Behind the fund sits the person the problem actually belongs to — the Yale researcher trying to move a discovery from the lab bench to the market. Their work is funded by research grants. A seed round, when it eventually comes, requires a company with a de-risked asset and a credible path to a clinic or a customer. Between those two things is a gap, and it is crossed almost entirely on non-dilutive federal funding: SBIR and STTR awards, and the translational and entrepreneurial programmes that individual agencies run alongside them.

Yale Ventures' job is to walk ventures across that gap. Doing it well means knowing, in advance, which federal programmes exist, what each one funds, how much it writes, at what stage it writes it, and whose name is on the door.

How they solve it today

Two ways, and both are bad.

Word of mouth. Someone who has been through an NIH SBIR tells you how it went. This is high-quality information with no coverage guarantee — you learn about the programmes your network happens to have touched, which systematically excludes the ones you most need to hear about.

Agency by agency, by hand. Open a federal agency's website. Hunt for its entrepreneurial or small-business arm. Read the solicitation. Try to infer check size and stage fit from a document written for applicants who already understand the programme. Dig for a programme officer's name. Repeat for the next agency, 30 to 100 times, because that is roughly how many federal funding bodies — large and small — are in scope.

What does not exist is an aggregated map of the federal funding ecosystem built for early-stage research spinouts. Grants.gov and SBIR.gov publish opportunities. Neither explains how the organizations publishing them relate to one another.

What it costs them

  • Time. At roughly 1.5 to 3 hours to build one usable agency profile by hand, a single pass across the ecosystem is four to nine weeks of analyst time — and it is stale by the time it is finished. Section 7 shows that estimate agreeing with what Yale Ventures reports it actually took.
  • Coverage. Manual sweeps miss organizations nobody on the team has heard of. The small, specialized programmes are the easiest to miss and often the least competitive to win — the misses are the expensive part.
  • Opacity. A website confirms that an institute exists. It does not tell you that the National Institute of Nursing Research sits inside NIH, draws on NIH's SBIR mechanism, sets its own priorities and budget, and has its own programme officer. The relationships are the thing that matters and the thing that is invisible.
  • Relationship lag. You cannot build a relationship with a programme officer in the week you need the money. Without a map, outreach is reactive — you meet the agency when you are already asking it for something.

Why anyone should care

Non-dilutive federal funding is the bridge between a grant-funded discovery and a venture-backable company. A spinout that crosses it arrives at its seed round with more data, a cleaner cap table and less dilution. A spinout that cannot find the bridge frequently does not arrive at all — the science is sound and the company never exists. Mapping the ecosystem is cheap. The ventures lost to not having mapped it are not.

3What Is New

Named existing products, honestly compared.

It is worth saying plainly at the outset: AI-powered grant matching is not new. At least four funded products already do it. What follows is what each covers and where the gap actually sits.

AlternativeWhat it does wellWhere it falls short
Granted AI Closest thing that exists. AI funder matching across 133K foundations, funder profiles listing key people, and real network-graph visualization. Foundation-centric; the 133K are foundations, not federal agencies. Its graphs show funder→recipient money flow, not an agency's organizational structure. No programme officer contacts.
Scout The only competitor naming tech transfer offices as a target user. Free AI matching across tens of thousands of solicitations. Organized entirely around the open solicitation and its deadline. No contact layer, no ecosystem mapping. It helps you apply; it cannot help you understand who you are applying to.
Candid Foundation Maps Profiles 170+ federal agencies and draws funder–recipient network graphs. Built for philanthropy. Maps who received money, not how agencies nest, and has no concept of a venture arm, check-size band or stage fit.

Grants.gov, SBIR.gov Authoritative and free; every federal opportunity is posted. Built for someone who already knows what to search. SBIR.gov covers SBIR/STTR only, missing ARPA-H, BARDA/DRIVe, CDMRP, DIU, In-Q-Tel and other venture arms.
NIH RePORTER, USAspending The definitive record of what was funded, by which institute. Backward-looking. Tells you what happened, never who to call.
Consultants (FreeMind, BBCetc, Grant Engine) Genuinely expert; they hold exactly the relationship knowledge this project captures. Priced per engagement, and the knowledge leaves when they do. The institution never accumulates a map.
ChatGPT, used directly Fast and fluent on any single question you think to ask. No persistent artefact, no coverage guarantee, and it invents programme officers and check sizes with total confidence.

Where the gap is. Searching for grant-matching AI returns a crowded field. Searching for a federal funding ecosystem map built for technology transfer returns general spending dashboards — the NSF/EDA place-based investment map, NYT university funding maps, RAND spending methodology — and no product built for this user at all. That asymmetry defines what is new here, and it is not the matching.

What Atlas does differently

  1. It maps organizations and their relationships, not opportunities. Every competitor is organized around the solicitation, which is a row with a deadline. Atlas is a graph: U.S. Federal Government → HHS → NIH → NINDS. That structure is what makes it visible that NINR sits inside NIH and shares its SBIR mechanism — a fact no solicitation list can express.
  2. It is scoped to venture and entrepreneurial arms. NIH does hundreds of things; Atlas indexes only the part that writes cheques to spinouts. Narrow scope is what makes completeness achievable.
  3. It carries a sourced contact layer — the field every competitor omits, and the one Yale Ventures leadership actually asked for.
  4. Two interfaces over one graph. The matcher is a commodity alone; on top of the map it returns the organizational context explaining why a programme fits.

Being honest. The matching chatbot alone would not be novel — Scout gives it away free. The claim is about the ecosystem layer and the contact data. And the hard part is not discovery but staleness: programme officers rotate and solicitations change. Every field carries its source and a retrieval date, and the app says "not published" rather than guessing. Section 6 covers how that is enforced; section 8 measures where it still fails.

4The Agents

One real run, traced step by step, with screenshots.

Atlas runs three language-model agents and two stages that could have been agents and are not, because deterministic code does the job better and for nothing. Which is which matters more than the count: the plain-Python stages are why a search costs seven cents.

StageWhat it doesModel call?When

Tools, and what is deliberately withheld

No agent chooses its own tools. Each gets fixed inputs and a schema it must fill, and cannot call out to anything. An agent that decides what to fetch is one whose cost and behaviour cannot be bounded in advance.

AgentToolsWithheld
Enrichment context_for() — the node's ancestors, siblings and children, so it can describe relationships; the Profile schema; the curator's note. Web access; any ability to add or re-parent a node.
Brief The ResearchBrief schema; the user's text in <research_description> delimiters. The graph. It does not know which programmes exist, so it cannot bias the brief toward one.
Ranking shortlist()'s twelve candidates; condense(); the MatchResult schema. The other 106 organizations, and all contacts and sources — it never sees a contact, so it cannot invent one.

How the pieces fit together

Handoffs. At build time seed.py asserts 118 organizations and how they nest; enrich.py hands each one, plus its ancestors and siblings, to the Enrichment Agent, which returns a Profile for a node a human placed — it cannot add, remove or re-parent one. At run time the map involves no agent at all, and the matching pipeline is a four-step relay: the Guard accepts or rejects (a rejection ends the request before a token is spent); the Brief Agent sees only the user's words; Retrieval returns twelve candidates with no model involved; the Ranking Agent ranks them, and its output is filtered against the shortlist so any node_id it invented is dropped.

Which model, and why

All three run on gpt-6-luna through Portkey. The course key permits only this model, so the honest answer is that it was the one available. What the constraint shaped was how it is used: structured output on every call, so an unparseable profile is retried rather than stored; output caps per stage (2,000 brief / 9,000 rank / 4,000 enrich), since output costs $10 per million against $1.25; a shared system prompt at build time that the cache serves back cheaply; and concurrency of eight. Given a free choice a smaller model would do the brief and the strongest would rank — each a one-line change.

One real run, traced

Recorded 26 October 2026; every number from data/usage_log.jsonl.

1. The description. A professor describes an AAV-delivered gene therapy targeting TYK2 for multiple sclerosis, with mouse efficacy data, no company formed, needing $2–3M over two years. 452 characters, no structure, no agency named.

2. The Guard clears it against nine injection patterns and the length bounds, in under a millisecond, at no cost.

3. The Brief Agent reads it. One call, 984 input and 521 output tokens, $0.0064. It returns modality gene therapy, indication multiple sclerosis, stage proof-of-concept, $2.5M over 24 months, and eleven keywords.

The keywords are where the design shows. The agent must emit both specific terms and broader category terms an agency would use about itself, so alongside "TYK2" it produced "neurology", "neurological disorders" and "central nervous system". Federal institutes never name a single disease: the institute that funds MS work calls itself the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke. An earlier prompt emitted only specific terms, and NINDS — the most relevant body in the map — did not make the shortlist at all.

4. Retrieval narrows 118 to 12. No model call, no cost, milliseconds. NINDS scores 35.2, NEI 30.8, NIAMS 27.6 — NEI because its focus areas say gene therapy, NIAMS because it funds autoimmune disease, NINDS because it covers neurological disorders. Three routes to the same patient, found by three different term matches. This step is also the cost control: all 118 profiles would be ~92,000 input tokens; twelve condensed ones are about 8,000.

5. The Ranking Agent decides. One call, 8,145 fresh input, 1,187 cached, 5,397 output, $0.0643, eleven ranked matches. NINDS came first at 88, naming the mechanism rather than restating the mission: MS is a nervous-system disorder, NINDS's remit — but the Blueprint Neurotherapeutics Network it lists funds small molecules, not gene therapy. On check size it refused to confirm a fit, because NINDS publishes none, rather than quoting an NIH-wide figure. And it flagged that SBIR requires an eligible small business and Yale itself cannot be the applicant — the most important fact for a researcher without a company.

6. Back to the map. The profile the agent reasoned over is the record the map shows. Clicking NINDS places it in the hierarchy — Federal Government › HHS › NIH › NINDS — with the same check sizes, timeline, contact and confidence. One graph, two readings.

Cost per user, and at scale

Cost scales with searches, not users. A Yale Ventures analyst at 20 searches/month costs $1.35; a tech transfer officer at 8, $0.54; a researcher at 3, $0.20. Blended at five searches, $0.34 per active user per month. A quarterly rebuild adds $0.81/month, flat at any scale.

What we did to keep cost down

Retrieve before ranking is the decisive one: scoring in Python and sending twelve condensed profiles instead of the whole catalogue. Build once and commit the artefact, so no user ever pays for enrichment and a fresh clone never rebuilds. The map calls no model. condense() strips contacts, sources and materials before ranking sees a profile — 71% of the text kept, and a safety property too. Output caps per stage. One shared system prompt at build time, worth $0.24. And the Guard rejects before spending, so a blocked request costs exactly $0.00.

Did the cost cutting make it worse? Yes — measurably

Shortlist size sets the cost of a search, so it is worth testing properly. experiments/shortlist_sweep.py runs two descriptions at five settings and records both halves of the trade. "Still right" is defined in advance: each case names the organizations a knowledgeable programme officer would expect — NINDS and NCATS for the MS therapy; ARPA-E, DOE SBIR/STTR and LEEP for the CO₂ catalyst.

Cutting from twelve to eight saves 35% and loses a real answer: DOE SBIR/STTR, the Department of Energy's own small-business programme, drops off the catalyst list entirely. A founder reading the cheap version would never learn it existed — the exact failure this project was built to fix, reintroduced to save two cents. Cutting to four saves 53% and breaks both cases. Spending more does not work at all: twenty candidates does not cost more, it fails, because the agent writes five prose fields per candidate and overruns the cap. The input bill was never the binding constraint; the output cap was, and it binds as a cliff.

What changed because of this. The MS search at twelve used 5,397 output tokens against a 6,000 cap — 90%. A wordier run would have shown the user an error. The cap was raised to 9,000, and run_match now walks a ladder — 12, then 8, then 6 — retrying with fewer candidates on an overrun and reporting the reduction in the trace. Twelve is a measured compromise, not a default.

6Safety

A screenshot or test of a defense actually working.

One risk outweighs the rest. The app tells a researcher which federal body to approach and who to contact, and people act on that; a wrong contact is only discovered after the outreach has been sent. Accuracy is not one safety concern among several here, it is the one the product lives on — so the defenses below are ordered by damage, not by how interesting the attack is.

Risk 1 — the agent invents a fact

What could be inventedDefense
An organization that does not exist Structural. All 118 nodes are asserted by hand in atlas/seed.py; the agent has no mechanism to add, remove or re-parent one.
A person — the costliest error, since officers rotate and a wrong name burns a first impression Policy plus audit. The prompt requires a programme inbox over an individual. Across 118 profiles and 82 contacts the build names zero individuals.
A citation — a URL or email that looks right Provenance filter in atlas/verify.py, applied at build time. This one caught a real defect.
A number — an award ceiling or budget Unknown values render as "not published", never a guessed range. Every profile carries a confidence rating and a note naming its weakest part.

The defense that caught something

check_accuracy.py was written to audit the shipped graph, not to pass, and it failed. Five citations sat on domains the profile never cited. Three were real addresses like sbir@nci.nih.gov that a reader could not confirm from the pages given. One was the genuine fabrication: navy-sbir-sttr.com, cited for the Office of Naval Research. The Navy's real SBIR site is navysbir.com, which is in the curated seed; the cited domain matches no organization anyone vouched for. The model had produced a domain that reads exactly like the real one.

The fix is a rule. atlas/verify.py keeps a citation only if it lands on a federal domain (.gov, .mil, .edu) or on a domain appearing as an official homepage in the seed. The second clause is necessary, not lax: many federal innovation arms are not on .gov — AFWERX is afwerx.com, In-Q-Tel iqt.org, SpaceWERX spacewerx.us — so a blanket ".gov only" rule would discard correct citations for exactly the organizations a spinout most needs. Anything failing both is stripped before storage, on every build.

Three lines deserve attention. Zero named individuals across 82 contacts is the policy holding on the field most likely to be invented. 17 of 118 profiles say the body has no venture arm matters because a model that always finds one is inventing them. And confidence is graded rather than uniformly high, with every lowered rating explained.

Without the structural defense. experiments/fabrication_probe.py fed the Enrichment Agent three fictitious bodies invented to sound like real neighbours — a National Institute of Quantum Medicine under NIH, an ARPA-B under Commerce, a SEEDWERX under Defense. All three were refused: no check sizes, no emails, no budgets. NIQM was answered with a citation to NIH's actual institute list as evidence no such institute appears on it. Two caveats: three probes on one model is evidence, not proof — which is why the node list lives in code, not a prompt — and the first run reported only 1 of 3 refusals because our own phrase list omitted "could not verify". The model was right; the instrument was wrong.

Risk 2 — attacks on the agent

Three layers. A pattern scan before any model call covering nine injection families: instruction override, role reassignment, prompt and credential extraction, forged role tags, jailbreak phrasings, ranking manipulation. Delimiter isolation, so the system prompt names the user's text as data. And nothing worth stealing — the Brief Agent has no tools, no graph access and no credentials in context.

check_guards.py runs ten cases — two legitimate, eight hostile or malformed — and all behave as expected at zero token cost. One was a real find: the ranking-manipulation pattern used \w+, which silently failed on every hyphenated agency name (ARPA-E, ARPA-H, SBIR/STTR — most of the ones worth promoting). The test caught it.

Risk 3 — malfunction

Returns an organization that is not real: every node_id is checked against the shortlist and unknown ids dropped. Invents a contact while ranking: impossible — condense() strips contacts before ranking sees a profile. Loops forever: there is no loop; two fixed calls, no agent picks its next step. Returns nothing: found in testing (section 5), fixed with the retry ladder. Unparseable output: retried, not stored. Quiet overconfidence: every profile shows its confidence, uncertainty note, sources and date.

Risk 4 — overspending

Five limits: a 6,000-character input cap; output caps per stage, the real ceiling since output is 86% of the bill; 20 requests per 5 minutes per IP; a $5.00 process spend ceiling as backstop, so a bug defeating the rate limit still cannot drain the key; and guard rejection before any call, at $0.00. The ceiling is tested in check_guards.py: at $0.03 of a $0.05 test ceiling it allows, at $0.06 it blocks.

What is still exposed

The pattern scan is a filter, not a proof — a novel phrasing will reach the model, which is tolerable only because the Brief Agent has nothing to give up. Profiles go stale and the app cannot tell. And the provenance filter checks the domain, not the page, so a dead link on a real federal domain still reaches the reader. Section 8 measures that rather than leaving it a worry: 18.5% of cited URLs return a 404.

Where the hours number comes from. "A couple of months" is a phrase, not a measurement, so it is checked two ways. From the pilot's estimate, two months of analyst time is 346 hours at full allocation or 173 at half. From the bottom up, building one usable profile by hand — finding the venture arm, inferring check size and stage, working out the cycle, hunting a contact — is 1.5 to 3 hours, so 118 organizations is 177 to 354 hours. The two overlap at 175–350 hours, four to nine weeks. At a loaded $53/hour (a ~$85,000 research-administrator salary plus 30% benefits over 2,080 hours) that is $9,000 to $18,700 to produce once, by hand, a map that is stale on arrival. Atlas produced it in 4 minutes 27 seconds for $2.45.

The larger, recurring number is misdirected applications. USDA's own SBIR guidance estimates ~150 hours over 12 weeks for a Phase I application; university sources say 150 to 450. NIH SBIR Phase I success has fallen to roughly 13%, from about 19% a few years earlier. Section 4's traced run is a concrete instance of the error avoided: the agent flagged that NINDS's Blueprint Neurotherapeutics Network funds small molecules, not gene therapy — a 150-hour application prevented by two sentences. Across 200 researchers, preventing two misdirected Phase I applications a year recovers 300 hours, about $15,900.

How many people this reaches. The pilot puts the portfolio at roughly 200 researchers; public Yale Ventures reporting is consistent, recording 331 invention disclosures, 225 patents issued and 11 faculty-led spinouts in FY2026 alone. If each of 200 researchers spends even two hours a year working out which programmes fit, that is 400 hours; reducing the search to under a minute removes roughly 300 hours, $15,900 a year — modelled, not measured.

What was not possible before. Two things. Relationship building ahead of need — the original question was not "where do we apply" but "who should we build relationships with before we need them", which a spreadsheet of twenty people with unknown titles cannot answer and a map of 101 venture arms with 82 sourced contacts can. And seeing what nobody mentions: Treasury's SSBCI, which routes federal capital through state venture programmes; EDA's Build to Scale, which funds the accelerator rather than the company; the DoD Office of Strategic Capital, which lends rather than grants; MTEC and MCDC, Other Transaction consortia far faster than a grant cycle; the FDA's CDRH TAP, free strategic advice on breakthrough devices. For scale, SBA's FY2022 report puts SBIR at $4.12 billion and STTR at $618 million across 11 agencies — and that is only the SBIR mechanism.

How good is this evidence. The strongest claims are the pilot's own: word of mouth, twenty people, unknown titles, a couple of months. Those describe a baseline, from the organization with the problem. The dollar conversions are weaker — $53/hour is a public aggregator average, not Yale's loaded cost, and the savings models rest on published effort estimates rather than observed behaviour change. What is missing is a researcher timed both ways and a count of applications whose destination actually changed. Neither exists yet, because the app is days old.

8Limits and Next Steps

What doesn't work yet

Depth predicts rot, and the model writes deep. Homepages fail 4.5% of the time; deep links 46.4%. A path like /medical-devices/cdrh-innovation/cdrh-total-product-life-cycle-tap-program is a guess at a page that may have been reorganised or may never have existed. (A further 10.7% of URLs are federal sites refusing automated requests — those pages are fine.)

Two things worth being blunt about. Six of the dead links are mine, hand-written into seed.py — ARPA-H's funding page, BARDA Ventures, NSF PFI, NIH I-Corps, DOE's LEEP and Loan Programs Office. Curation is no defense against link rot. And the damage lands exactly where section 6 located the accuracy guarantee: 11 of 66 contact verification links are dead, including NINDS — the top-ranked match in the traced run. The amber banner tells users to confirm on the official page, and for one contact in six that page 404s. Four organizations now have no live source at all.

Smaller gaps, stated without elaboration: annual_budget_usd is 0 for all 118 organizations — a schema field the model never once filled; 31 organizations publish no check size and 37 carry no contact; nothing ages or is re-checked; retrieval is validated on only two domains (biomedical and energy); the matcher is single-turn and cannot ask the clarifying question it already knows it needs; and there are no accounts, so a tech transfer officer cannot save a shortlist or record an outreach.

What I would build next: verify every citation at build time

Fetch every URL during the build, and refuse to store a citation that does not resolve.

The checking code already exists — experiments/link_check.py is the prototype. It needs to move into atlas/verify.py and run inside sanitize() on every build, beside the domain rule already there:

  1. After a profile is generated, fetch each URL it cites. The build is already concurrent at eight workers; this adds about two minutes to a four-minute build and costs nothing in tokens.
  2. A URL returning 404 is dropped, exactly as an off-domain URL is dropped today.
  3. If that leaves a profile with no live source, fall back to the organization's homepage — which resolves 95.5% of the time — and lower the profile's confidence to reflect that the specific page could not be confirmed.
  4. Record the HTTP status and check date against each URL and show them beside the existing confidence rating, so a citation becomes a claim with a verification date rather than a hyperlink.
  5. Run the same check on a schedule against the committed graph, so rot appearing after the build is caught rather than discovered by a user.

The effect is measurable in advance. Today 69% of cited URLs resolve; replacing every dead deep link with the organization's homepage would put live-citation coverage at roughly 95%, because that is the rate homepages already achieve.
